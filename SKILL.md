# Travel Planner

Create travel plans that are specific enough to follow on the day. Optimize the trip around the traveler's real constraints, not an abstract list of famous places. A good itinerary balances landmark coverage, natural scenery, food, walking, rest, transfers, cost, and the risk of missing fixed transport.

## Working principles

- Treat booked flights, lodging, purchased tickets, and fixed rail/ferry departures as anchors. Plan the day around them and never quietly move them to make the route look tidy.
- Respect the traveler's stated priorities. When unstated, ask whether nature, museums, food, nightlife, photography, shopping, or a slower pace matters most.
- Judge a detour by what it adds, not distance alone. Keep a worthwhile viewpoint, coast, historic quarter, distinctive architecture, or memorable walk even when it adds travel. Remove low-value filler and unrequested shopping. Keep shopping only when the traveler wants it or it is a named goal such as a particular store.
- Treat lodging as part of the route. Include arrival, luggage drop, check-in, checkout, and bag collection at the times they affect movement. Do not assume luggage storage is free or available; honor a preference to leave bags at lodging and avoid paid lockers where practical.
- Make the schedule intense only when the traveler asks for that pace. A dense plan still needs realistic transfers, queues, meal windows, daylight, and fallback choices.
- Use the traveler's language, units, dietary preferences, currency, and preferred level of detail.

## 1. Build a constraint ledger

Read the conversation and provided files first. Record what is already known before asking anything. Track each important item as **confirmed**, **user-stated but unchecked**, **proposed**, or **needs verification**. If dates are in the past or the year conflicts with a supplied booking, clarify before treating flights and schedules as current.

Capture, when relevant:

- Travel dates, year, weekdays, time zones, city order, and overnight transit.
- Flight/train/ferry numbers and times, terminals/stations/ports, airline, cabin/fare, baggage allowance, check-in preference, and ticket status.
- Lodging name and exact address, booking dates, luggage drop/collection rules, check-in/out times, and whether bags can stay there.
- Passport/visa/entry requirements only to the level needed for the itinerary; do not infer eligibility from nationality alone.
- Pace, walking tolerance, accessibility needs, mobility, sleep, natural/urban priorities, photography, shopping, must-sees, and acceptable skips.
- Number and style of meals, cuisine preferences, allergies/restrictions, alcohol preferences, per-meal or daily budget, and tipping assumptions.
- Food preferences and exclusions such as preferred proteins, cheese, vegetarian choices, or unfamiliar ingredients the traveler rejects. Notice repeated cuisine across neighboring days.
- Specific shops or brands the traveler explicitly wants to visit; find the exact branch rather than listing only the brand.
- Total budget, payment method, cash needs, desired currency, and whether prices should be converted to the home currency.
- Desired output: concise schedule, detailed guide, linked document, PDF, map, or some combination.

Ask only for missing facts that materially change routing or feasibility. Bundle essential questions. Continue independent research and drafting while waiting. If dates, addresses, ticket conditions, or flight details are not available, label the assumption and avoid presenting it as confirmed.

## 2. Research facts that change the plan

Use live web/search tools when available. Prefer official sources for operational facts: airline and airport pages, transit agencies, rail/ferry operators, attraction ticket pages, venue hours, restaurant menus/reservations, and lodging policies. Use reputable maps and current local sources for walking and transit estimates. Record source links and the date checked for facts likely to change.

Verify the following where they affect the itinerary:

- Local opening hours on the exact weekday and season, holidays, last entry, timed-entry requirements, closures, and seasonal operations.
- The actual requested-year train, ferry, and flight schedule when published. If not published, use current patterns only as a planning estimate and mark them provisional.
- Which stations, terminals, piers, entrances, and airport buildings are meant. Use the full searchable place name and address when ambiguity is likely.
- Transit route, transfer count, walk to/from the stop, fare, fare cap, airport surcharge, payment method, and whether advance purchase changes the price or only guarantees a seat.
- Tickets, entry windows, prices, tax/fees, refunds, age or residency conditions, and official purchase channel. Do not call a ticket “booked” because a price or product was seen.
- Restaurant hours, service style, menu item and current price when possible, reservation requirements, kitchen closing time, tax, service charge, and likely tip.
- Seasonal daylight, weather norms, daylight-sensitive photo stops, and conditions that can close outdoor facilities.

Separate facts from estimates. Cite primary sources in the guide when useful. If browsing is unavailable, say so and give a verification checklist instead of inventing current prices or timetables.

## 3. Design the route around real anchors

First group attractions geographically and identify the high-value natural and cultural anchors. Then connect them to the lodging, meals, and fixed transport.

- Include the hotel/hostel, airport, major station, and port as itinerary destinations whenever the traveler must go there, leave bags, or transfer.
- For each day, plan the route from lodging and back to lodging or onward transport. Show where a bag is dropped, collected, and carried.
- Avoid needless backtracking, but keep a scenic or iconic detour when its experience is worth the extra walk or transit. Explain the choice briefly when it adds meaningful time.
- Remove redundant viewpoints or repetitive districts only when they add little beyond a nearby stronger stop. Do not remove an attraction merely because it is not on the shortest route.
- Give city viewpoints, parks, historic streets, waterfronts, and photo locations their right-sized time. A simple photo stop may take 5–15 minutes; an enjoyable coastline, waterfront, or scenic walk can include sitting, resting, and looking around. Never compress a place the traveler wants to enjoy into a drive-by photo.
- Treat shopping as a separate activity. Do not fill time with malls or retail streets when the user says shopping is not a goal. Keep requested stores, markets, or shopping only when they offer a distinct cultural/architectural experience or are explicitly requested.
- Do not let a restaurant, exchange office, or optional attraction break a fixed ferry, rail, or flight connection.
- Add a fallback for weather, closure, delay, fatigue, or a missed optional booking. State what to drop first and what must remain protected.

### Walking and transit logic

Use walking estimates that include street crossings, entrances, hills, weather, and photo pauses where material. Use map routing if available; otherwise label estimates as approximate. Keep walking chains geographically coherent.

Use an arrow (`A → B → C`) only for a continuous walk. When a bus, train, metro, rideshare, ferry, or other vehicle is needed, start a new timed row and name the mode, line/route, direction, transfer, and destination. For high-frequency local transit, concise route instructions are enough. For major transport, give the exact operator, station/terminal/pier, target departure or arrival, purchase method, and relevant buffer.

## 4. Build a realistic time plan

Allocate time for the complete movement and visit, not just the attraction. Make each time block internally possible:

- Account for landing, immigration, airport transfer, bag drop, and hotel distance on arrival days.
- On departure days, work backward from the airline's actual check-in/document-check cutoff, security, immigration or U.S. preclearance, terminal walking/shuttle, boarding time, and the traveler's chosen gate-arrival target.
- Do not use a generic “arrive 2 or 3 hours early” rule without checking the airport, airline, route, terminal, counter opening/cutoff, queue patterns, and the traveler's bag/check-in situation. No checked bag does not remove a counter document check or security queue.
- Keep a distinction between **leave lodging**, **arrive at airport**, **counter closes**, and **arrive at gate**. A desired gate target is subordinate to published airline deadlines and real airport operations.
- Schedule meals at the point they naturally fall between morning/afternoon or afternoon/evening blocks. Avoid a restaurant that requires a large detour, long queue, or a missed transport connection.
- Match the traveler's real meal count; do not add breakfast or a third meal by habit. If they specify a simple lunch and a proper dinner, keep lunch quick and inexpensive and reserve the main local-food choice for dinner.
- Add a practical buffer where uncertainty is high; identify the consequence of a delay and the first optional stop to drop.
- Use local time and clearly mark date-line crossings and overnight flights.

## 5. Plan meals to the request

Choose recognizable, well-regarded local food that fits the traveler's route, budget, hours, and dietary preferences. “Local” does not mean unusual ingredients the traveler has rejected. Prefer places with a real reason to visit; do not add famous but expensive restaurants just for prestige.

For each meal, give:

- Searchable restaurant/branch name and a map/search link when available.
- What to order, with a reasonable fallback if sold out or closed.
- Price in local currency and estimated home-currency equivalent.
- Whether tax, card fees, service charge, and expected tip are included in the estimate.
- A reservation/queue note only when it changes the plan.

Prefer named, searchable restaurant branches over vague suggestions such as “find a noodle shop nearby.” If live sources are available, check that the branch serves the proposed dish and is open in the planned window; otherwise label it as a candidate and give a nearby same-style backup. Local food does not mean unusual ingredients the traveler has rejected. When cheese is a stated priority, include a well-reviewed, route-compatible cheese-forward option such as pizza or a cheeseburger. For non-drinkers, do not recommend alcohol; flag beer batter, wine sauces, or alcohol-based marinades as questions to ask when recipe details are unclear.

Respect meal count. If the traveler eats two meals, do not quietly add breakfast; if a flight meal replaces lunch or dinner, say so. For daily budgets, total the meals honestly and flag days near or over the cap. Do not label a meal “under budget” while excluding mandatory service/tip assumptions.

For alcohol avoidance, identify uncertain alcohol in batter, sauces, marinades, gravy, and desserts where relevant; advise asking the venue rather than guaranteeing an unverified recipe. Never imply an allergy is safe without venue confirmation.

## 6. Make bookings and transport actionable

Provide a booking list separate from the day-by-day route. Include only items that need advance booking or are materially easier/cheaper to buy ahead; distinguish them from walk-up transit.

Use a status vocabulary consistently:

- **已购 / 已订**: traveler explicitly confirms purchase or a booking record shows it.
- **建议预订**: official/current evidence supports advance purchase.
- **可现场购买**: current source indicates day-of purchase is practical.
- **待核 / 未开放 / 暂排**: schedule, product, price, or date is not confirmed.
- **备选**: only used if the main option fails.

For each booking, provide the exact event/date, official or requested sales channel, when to buy, price basis, change/cancellation condition if relevant, and what evidence is still missing. Include purchased transport even if no further action is needed so the traveler sees it in the timeline. Never assume an aggregator product matches a similarly named official service without checking operator, duration, pier, inclusions, and timing.

Give a transport budget by city/day or leg. Include airport connections, transit fares, ferries, intercity journeys, and likely card/cash fees. Identify fare caps, airport surcharges, and what is excluded. Use the user's currency and state the exchange-rate assumption/date.

When asked to plan cash or foreign exchange, compare card acceptance, ATM operator and issuer fees, exchange-office rate/fee transparency, and the need for small local notes. Route a named exchange office only after confirming its exact branch and current operation; do not promise a “best rate” without comparable quotes. Keep cash needs modest and separate them from card spending. Explain customary tip amounts, payment timing, and card-terminal handling using current local guidance.

When flight details are supplied and the traveler asks, make a per-leg airline table covering fare-family baggage limits, online check-in and seat selection, counter/document-check cutoffs, meals, connectivity, and notable cabin amenities. Use the actual ticket's fare-specific policy. For window-seat scenery, compare route direction, coastline/mountains, time of day, and aircraft/approach uncertainty; label the recommendation as probabilistic.

## 7. Present the itinerary clearly

For a detailed day-by-day guide, use the structure in [references/output-template.md](references/output-template.md). In summary:

- Organize each date into **早上（午餐前）／午餐／下午（晚餐前）／晚餐／晚上** as appropriate. If the traveler wants only morning/afternoon/evening, use their preferred grouping while keeping meals clearly inserted.
- Put a time range at the start of every route row.
- Chain only walkable consecutive stops with arrows. Put a vehicle transfer on its own line with route details.
- Name every stop so it can be searched in Google Maps. Add the relevant station, terminal, pier, hostel, or airport address when useful.
- Keep explanatory prose short, but include what makes the stop worth visiting, what to see/do, and how long to allow.
- Separate high-priority stops from optional/backup stops. Use a clear “if delayed, skip X first” note.
- Include booking checklist, transport budget, daily meal estimates, lodging/baggage logistics, practical airport guidance, and seasonal/weather notes when relevant.
- Include each specifically requested store as a searchable branch and route it in the right time window.
- A map is optional. Do not generate a map by default if it would be hard to read; use clear searchable place names instead.

If the user requests a PDF, create a readable, paginated PDF and also retain an editable source when practical. Prefer legible spacing over forcing a page count. Include working links. Check the rendered page count, overflow/clipping, page breaks, and key text after generation. Keep only the requested final version when the user asks to remove older drafts; do not delete research sources or unrelated files.

## 8. Final quality audit

Before presenting the plan, check it against [references/planning-checklist.md](references/planning-checklist.md). At minimum confirm:

- Dates, weekdays, time zones, flights, stations, lodging addresses, ticket times, and city order agree everywhere.
- Every day starts from a real place and ends at the right lodging/transport; bag movements are possible.
- Walking arrows do not hide a transit leg; time windows cover both transit and visit.
- Operating hours and seasonal availability support the plan, or the uncertainty is visible.
- No unrequested shopping filler remains; worthwhile scenic and landmark detours stay in.
- Photo-only stops are brief where appropriate, while scenery/waterfront time matches the traveler's intent.
- Meal count, dietary preferences, budget math, tax/tip assumptions, and restaurant hours are consistent.
- Every purchased item is described as purchased only with confirmation; tentative products and schedules remain tentative.
- Airport targets are specific to the flight and airport, with counter deadline and gate target distinguished.
- Requested airline details match the actual route and fare family; named shop branches and food preferences appear in the plan.
- The guide has clear fallbacks and does not pretend uncertain schedules are confirmed.

End with the practical result and the most important unresolved checks, not a generic list of travel advice.
